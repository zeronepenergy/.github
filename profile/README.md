## ✨ Welcome to Zero Nep Energy

# Zero Nep Energy

*Reliable, decentralized electricity for every community in Nigeria.*

## The Vision

Electricity is a basic amenity. We build decentralized power systems that bring communities and regions to 24/7 electricity.

## Who We Are

We are a team of researchers and scientists working on Nigeria's electricity infrastructure. We grew up shouting "Up NEPA!" when the lights came on, and some of us even prayed for NEPA/PHCN to restore power. We know the problem personally, and we are building the solution.

## Why Now

Under the new Electricity Act, states and individuals can now participate directly in the power sector, so communities no longer have to wait on the national grid. Funding is also moving toward off-grid solutions: more than 1,000 mini-grids are under development under the World Bank-backed $750 million DARES programme \[3\], and the World Bank's Mission 300 plans to bring electricity to 17.5 million Nigerians mainly through off-grid and renewable solutions \[4\].

## The Problem

**Nigeria has the largest electricity access gap in the world.**

- About 86.8 million Nigerians were without electricity, according to the World Bank's SDG7 tracking report \[1\].
- Other estimates are higher: a 2026 case study puts national access at 62.5%, leaving roughly 100 million people without power \[2\].

**The grid delivers a fraction of what exists on paper.**

- Installed capacity is over 15,500 MW, but the grid can only wheel around 4,500 MW \[5\].

**Nigerians fill the gap with generators, at enormous cost.**

- An estimated $23 billion a year is spent on diesel and petrol across about 22 million generators \[5\]. A lower estimate from the IMF puts petrol-for-generators spending at $14 billion a year \[6\].
- The Federal Government said Nigerians spent N16.5 trillion on self-generation in 2023, against about N1 trillion in revenue for the formal power sector \[7\].
- Generators account for 48.6% of the electricity consumed in Nigeria, according to the NBS \[8\].
- Petrol-generator power costs about N450/kWh and diesel more than N900/kWh, compared with N206/kWh for homes on grid Band A \[7\].

**Industry and institutions pay the price.**

- Manufacturers' spending on alternative power rose 42.3% to N1.11 trillion in 2024 \[6\].
- The African Development Bank estimates outages cost firms about 3% of annual sales, and 70.7% of Nigerian businesses own or share a generator \[5\].

*Figures vary by source and year. Confirm the latest numbers before external use.*

## Resources and the Right Technology for Each Region

Nigeria's regions differ in sun, water, biomass and fuel resources, so we match the technology to the resource instead of using one design everywhere. This is our desk-research starting point, to be validated with on-site measurements before any deployment.

**Solar potential falls into three bands \[9\]:**

- Zone I (5.7 to 6.5 kWh/m²/day): Borno, Yobe, Jigawa, Kano, Kaduna, Bauchi, Gombe, Adamawa, Plateau and Katsina.
- Zone II (5.0 to 5.7): Sokoto, Zamfara, Kebbi, Niger, FCT, Nasarawa, Taraba and Kwara.
- Zone III (below 5.0): the southern states.

### North West (Kano, Kaduna, Katsina, Sokoto, Zamfara, Kebbi, Jigawa)

- **Resources:** strong sun and small-hydro sites. Surveyed small-hydro potential includes Kaduna (59.2 MW), Kano (46.2 MW), Sokoto (30.6 MW) and Katsina (8 MW) \[11\].
- **Best-fit technology:** solar PV with battery storage as the base, and small hydro where rivers allow (Kaduna, Kano).

### North East (Borno, Yobe, Adamawa, Bauchi, Gombe, Taraba)

- **Resources:** the highest solar band, small hydro, biomass and waste. One regional study found all six states viable for biomass and municipal solid waste power \[10\]. Surveyed small-hydro potential includes the Gongola basin (162.7 MW), Bauchi (42.6 MW) and Borno (20.8 MW) \[11\]. Hydro is already proven here: Waya Dam in Bauchi has installed turbines and Dadin Kowa Dam in Gombe has a 34 MW plant \[14\].
- **Best-fit technology:** solar and storage mini-grids, small hydro (Taraba, Bauchi, Gombe, Adamawa), and biomass or waste-to-energy as a complement.

### North Central (Plateau, Niger, Benue, Kogi, Kwara, Nasarawa, FCT)

- **Resources:** the richest small-hydro zone. Surveyed potential includes Niger (117.6 MW), Plateau (110.4 MW), Benue (69.2 MW) and Kwara (38.8 MW) \[11\]. One assessment estimates 3,500 MW of small-hydro potential across the zone \[15\]. NESCO already runs about 19 MW of small hydro around Jos \[16\]. Coal is also present: Kogi has eight coal blocks, and Benue and Nasarawa have two each \[17\].
- **Best-fit technology:** small hydro combined with solar. Coal is present but not recommended for small clean systems.
- **Battery supply chain:** lithium appears in Nasarawa's mineral production data \[19\], which could matter later for local battery supply.

### South West (Lagos, Ogun, Oyo, Osun, Ondo, Ekiti)

- **Resources:** moderate sun, some coastal wind, and scattered small-hydro sites. UNIDO's survey lists potential sites in Ondo, Ekiti, Osun, Ogun and Oyo \[13\]. One satellite-based study found Lagos wind power density above the on-grid threshold \[20\].
- **Best-fit technology:** solar plus battery for universities and institutions, biogas or waste-to-energy in dense areas, and wind-solar hybrids in the coastal belt once measured.

### South East (Enugu, Anambra, Imo, Abia, Ebonyi)

- **Resources:** coal, some oil and gas, and limited hydro. Enugu has six coal blocks, and Imo, Anambra and Ebonyi have one each \[17\]. Anambra is flagged for small-hydro potential \[23\].
- **Best-fit technology:** solar-battery hybrids, biogas, and small hydro where sites exist.

### South South (Rivers, Bayelsa, Delta, Akwa Ibom, Cross River, Edo)

- **Resources:** oil and gas, a strong hydro site in Cross River, and weak inland wind. Bayelsa is listed with oil and gas deposits \[22\]. Cross River sites include Agbokim Falls (about 20,000 kW) \[13\] and Kwa Falls Dam (about 10,000 kW) \[13\]. Delta State is a low-wind region, with power density under 100 W/m² \[21\].
- **Best-fit technology:** gas-fed embedded generation as a bridge, solar hybrids, small hydro (Cross River, Edo), and waste or biomass.

### Across all zones

One hybrid-system study found that a biomass generator, solar PV and battery combination was the best option for all six geopolitical zones \[9b\]. Our baseline is therefore solar plus storage, with biomass or hydro added where local resources allow.

### The scale of the opportunity

Nigeria has 278 potential small-hydro sites totalling 734.3 MW, but only about 37 MW has been developed \[12\]. This is a large and largely untouched opportunity for decentralized power.

## How We Work

Our solution is decentralized: every community or institution should have its own power supply.

- **Underserved and remote communities:** we deploy to rural and sparsely populated areas the national grid has long neglected.
- **Universities and institutions:** many run on generators for hours each day. Our systems cut their fuel costs, maintenance burden and downtime.
- **Local conditions first:** every deployment is designed around the local resource mix described above.

## Where We Are

1. **Research:** studying the energy needs and resources of each region, with the goal of deploying across all 744 local government areas.
2. **Education:** teaching Nigerians about the alternative forms of energy generation available to them.
